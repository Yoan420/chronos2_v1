Let me start with the price move. In August, the story was really about geopolitics and logistics, much more than demand. Brent came into the month at just over $90 per barrel. It then dropped quite sharply, to around $78 on 5 August, as the market became more optimistic about possible diplomatic progress involving Iran and Oman. But that didn't last. Tensions picked up again, Brent climbed to almost $95 on 21 August, and by the end of the month it was back around $90. So, after all that volatility, Brent finished more or less where it started.

Now, the tricky part is that we still don't really know how much oil is moving through the Strait of Hormuz. Depending on the source, the numbers are completely different. US authorities and Goldman Sachs are talking about roughly 8 to 15 million barrels per day, while ship-tracking services suggest something closer to 2 to 6 million. That's a very wide range. The main reason is that some vessels switch off their AIS signals, and some barrels move through ship-to-ship transfers, so they are very difficult to track. For this slide, we're using roughly 4.9 million barrels per day, which is close to the IEA's adjusted estimate for the second quarter. And this uncertainty matters. If the market really believed that flows were already back near the top of the range, we would probably be seeing lower prices.

Then there's the product market. The Houthi blockade has forced some Saudi barrels to take alternative routes through the East-West pipeline, Suez and the SUMED system. In practical terms, that can add three to four weeks to some journeys to Asia. On top of that, Russian refinery output was around 30% below its 2025 level in July, and repeated Ukrainian attacks pushed Russia to restrict diesel exports. Put all of that together, and it helps explain why diesel and other refined products have risen more strongly than crude itself.

On the demand side, it's a rather different story. Chinese refinery runs and apparent oil demand are still well below last year's levels. And in the US, the very large 17.4 million-barrel increase in commercial crude inventories cooled the price rally in mid-August. But the important point is that, even with softer demand, the overall market balance still looks tight. The IEA and EGM estimates point to stock draws of around 1.8 million barrels per day in the third quarter and 1.2 million in the fourth quarter. In other words, the deficit gets smaller, but it doesn't disappear.

So, if I had to sum it up, crude prices are likely to remain firm, and product margins could stay elevated through the end of the year. The main downside risk would be a credible political agreement on Hormuz. On the other hand, renewed attacks, further disruption to Russian refining, or a shortage of refined products could push prices higher again.
