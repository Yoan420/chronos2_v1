If I had to sum up the slide in one sentence, I would say that we still do not see a short-term way out of the crisis, but an uncontrolled escalation is no longer the most likely scenario either.

The renewed fighting is obviously keeping a geopolitical premium in crude. But the political picture has changed. The US did not get the quick outcome it was looking for, so the strategy now seems to be more about putting sustained financial pressure on Iran. That strategy already has limits, because China is unlikely to follow the US line and Beijing has leverage of its own through rare earths. On the Iranian side, there is also little urgency to compromise. The authorities can wait and see whether US policy changes after the November midterms. So the most likely scenario is a long stand-off: crude prices remain high, but without the extreme spike we would expect from a full regional escalation.

The more immediate pressure is actually in the product market. The chart on the lower left shows that jet and ICE gasoil cracks are now around 70 to 80 dollars per barrel above Brent, while the gasoline crack is close to 50 dollars. Before the conflict, these cracks were generally closer to 15 to 25 dollars. At current levels, that means diesel and jet fuel are trading at close to twice the price of crude, compared with roughly 30% above crude before the war.

The second chart shows why this matters. US distillate stocks have fallen to around 103 million barrels. That is roughly 20% below their January level and below the normal seasonal range. The US has been an important source of supply for the rest of the market, but with inventories this tight, its ability to maintain exports is becoming more limited. That increases the risk of further pressure in Europe and Asia, unless China decides to raise diesel exports substantially.

So the central message is quite simple. We are not expecting a quick political resolution, and we are not expecting an extreme crude-price spike in the base case. Instead, crude remains durably expensive, while the strongest upside risk is concentrated in diesel, jet fuel and gasoline. There is also a macro consequence: if energy and transport costs remain high, they slow the disinflation process and make it harder for interest rates to come down.
