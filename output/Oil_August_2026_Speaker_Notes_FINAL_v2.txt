If I had to sum up this slide in one sentence, I would say that there is still no short-term way out of the crisis, but the most likely scenario is now one of prolonged and contained tension, rather than an uncontrolled escalation. That means crude prices are likely to remain high, while the greatest upside risk is increasingly concentrated in refined products.

Let me start with the price dynamics. The chart at the top shows just how closely Brent has followed the shifts between military escalation and diplomatic hopes since the conflict began. More recently, prices fell below 80 dollars per barrel when the market became more optimistic about a possible agreement, before rebounding toward 95 dollars as hostilities resumed. Escalation risks now appear more limited, but a near-term political settlement remains unlikely. The US has shifted toward a strategy of sustained economic pressure on Iran, although that strategy has clear limits because China is unlikely to comply fully. On the Iranian side, there is also little urgency to compromise, as the authorities can wait to see whether US policy changes after the November midterms. So, overall, the probability of durably elevated prices without an extreme spike has increased.

The physical market is still very difficult to assess. US authorities and Goldman Sachs estimate that between 8 and 15 million barrels per day are moving through Hormuz, while ship-tracking services see only around 4 to 6 million. That is a very wide range, and all of these estimates remain well below the roughly 20 million barrels per day seen before the war. The difference partly reflects the difficulty of tracking vessels operating without normal identification signals, but it also means that the market cannot be certain about how much supply is actually available.

The more immediate pressure is in refined products. The chart on the lower left shows that diesel and jet-fuel premiums over Brent have risen from around 20 dollars per barrel before the conflict to as much as 80 dollars today. This is not only a consequence of lower Gulf exports. Ukrainian attacks have also affected roughly one-third of Russian refining capacity, while China temporarily halted its product exports before gradually resuming them. So even when crude is available, the capacity to turn it into diesel, jet fuel and gasoline — and to deliver those products where they are needed — remains constrained.

The chart on the lower right shows why the United States is so important, but also why its room for manoeuvre is narrowing. US refiners are operating at close to full capacity and have been supporting Europe and Asia through strong exports. At the same time, domestic distillate stocks have fallen sharply and are now below their normal seasonal range. This means US refiners cannot continue filling the global supply gap indefinitely without creating even more tension in their own market.

So the main conclusion is fairly simple. We do not expect a rapid end to the crisis, but a major escalation is no longer our central scenario either. Crude should therefore remain expensive without necessarily returning to its previous peaks, while diesel, jet fuel and gasoline remain exposed to further increases. If US exports begin to fall, the pressure will be felt particularly strongly in Europe and Asia. And there is a clear macro consequence: persistently high energy and transport costs will slow the disinflation process and keep upward pressure on interest rates.
