If I had to sum up the slide in one sentence, I would say that we still do not see a short-term way out of the crisis, but we also do not think an uncontrolled escalation is the most likely scenario anymore.

The renewed fighting is obviously keeping a geopolitical premium in crude. But the political picture has changed a little. The US did not get the quick result it was looking for, so the strategy now seems to be more about putting sustained financial pressure on Iran. The problem is that this strategy has limits. China is unlikely to follow the US line, and Beijing has leverage of its own through rare earths. On the Iranian side, there is also little urgency to compromise, because the authorities can wait and see whether US policy changes after the November midterms. So the most likely scenario is a long stand-off: prices stay high, but without the very sharp spike that a full regional escalation would produce.

The more immediate concern is actually the product market. In the week ending 21 August, New York diesel was trading at just over twice the price of Brent, and US Gulf Coast jet fuel at around 1.75 times Brent. In January and February, those ratios were only about 1.46 and 1.31. So the premium on products has widened very significantly.

At the same time, US inventories are becoming uncomfortably low. Distillate stocks were down around 20% from early January by 21 August, and gasoline stocks were down roughly 15%. The US has been an important source of supply for the rest of the market, but with inventories this tight, its capacity to keep exporting at the same pace is narrowing. That is particularly important for Europe and Asia, unless China decides to increase diesel exports quite substantially.

So the central message is fairly simple. Crude prices are likely to remain elevated because there is no political resolution in sight. But the strongest upside risk is now in diesel, jet fuel and gasoline, where supply is much less comfortable. And there is a macro consequence as well: if energy and transport costs remain high, they slow the disinflation process and make it harder for interest rates to come down.
